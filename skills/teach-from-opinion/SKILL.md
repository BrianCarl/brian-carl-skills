# Opinion to Education

Preserve the user's perspective. Make its practical value usable by the audience. Treat an opinion as a starting point, not an error to remove.

Use this test: **Could someone agree with the content and still have no idea how to use it?** If yes, identify what is missing before polishing the wording.

## 1. Find the teaching kernel

Read the supplied idea and available audience/format context. Avoid asking for information already supplied. Use known context; otherwise state a provisional assumption or ask only if it changes the lesson.

Extract:
- **Point of view:** What does the user believe, in their own terms?
- **Practical kernel:** What action, choice, diagnosis, or explanation is already present?
- **Learning outcome:** Complete “After this, the audience can ___.” Use an observable ability: choose, identify, compare, diagnose, perform, or explain a causal relationship. Avoid “understand the importance of.”
- **Missing specificity:** What does the learner need to apply the idea to a new situation?

If the takeaway itself is unclear, first identify the audience situation and the mistake, friction, or decision the content should address. Propose one likely takeaway; offer at most two alternatives when they would produce materially different lessons. Ask which problem the user actually intends to solve before elaborating a framework. Do not treat the user's categories or tools as the learning outcome.

Distinguish three gaps: no clear takeaway, a clear takeaway with missing application, and sufficient teaching that needs presentation work. Fix the earliest gap first. Do not polish instructions before deciding what they should help the learner accomplish.

Select one main outcome for short content. For long-form content, retain the article's broader promise and connect supporting lessons to it; do not silently turn an explanatory article into a narrow tutorial. Label a proposed practical exercise as an addition when it narrows the scope. Do not force every idea into a how-to; a diagnostic, decision rule, worked comparison, or causal explanation can be education.

When reviewing existing content, read the full source, identify teaching that already works, and recommend the smallest useful improvement. Do not assume an opinion-led piece lacks education. Distinguish explanatory value from procedural value; some pieces legitimately prioritize the former.

## 2. Check the premise

Check whether the claim is overbroad, contradicts its examples, confuses correlation with causation, or treats a personal heuristic as a universal rule. Explain a consequential issue plainly and propose a faithful refinement. Label it as a proposal if it changes the user's meaning.

Separate an unsupported premise from missing instruction. Do not manufacture steps to make a weak claim look teachable. Verify factual or technical claims through appropriate sources when necessary; do not research merely to replace the user's perspective with generic advice.

Keep factual corrections focused on claims that materially affect the lesson or proposed action. Distinguish a clearer explanation, a plausible tactic to test, and a demonstrated performance improvement. Do not turn an editorial review into an unrelated technical audit or imply that a cleaner example proves business results.

## 3. Use the hybrid workflow

Propose the likely takeaway first, then expose the precise gap. Use a compact response:
- “Your point is ...”
- “The practical lesson I see is ...”
- “The part we still need to teach is ...”

Choose the next move:

| Source state | Next move |
|---|---|
| No clear takeaway | Identify the audience problem, propose a likely outcome, and clarify the intended change. |
| Practical kernel, vague instruction | Retain the kernel and ask for criteria, a first move, or a demonstration. |
| Sufficient method and example | Draft directly; do not impose an interview. |
| Questionable premise | Explain the issue and propose a narrower lesson before constructing instruction. |

Ask one to three targeted questions per round, preferably one or two. Ask about missing substance, not broad content preferences. Tie each question to the idea. Choose only the question whose answer most changes the teaching:
- “What would someone look at to decide between these two options?”
- “Which mistake are you trying to help the audience avoid?”
- “If I followed that advice tomorrow, what would I do first, and what would I produce?”
- “Walk me through a real situation where you made this choice. What changed your decision?”
- “How would someone know they had done this well enough?”
- “When would your advice stop being useful?”

Offer a concrete candidate rule when helpful so the user can react to it. Distinguish that proposal from their established view.

When a missing answer determines the substance, leave a useful provisional lesson or outline and ask the targeted question before finalizing that part. Complete independent review and improvements now; do not block the entire task on one unknown. When the answer only changes presentation, proceed using a stated assumption. If the user explicitly requests a best-effort draft without questions, draft and briefly label substantive assumptions. Treat requests to review or test the skill on an article as reviews, not automatic permission to rewrite the entire article.

Do not invent the user's experience, results, decision rules, preferences, or examples. Label illustrative examples as hypothetical when their origin could be mistaken. Retain uncertainty rather than disguising it with confident instructional copy.

## 4. Build the lesson

Use the lightest structure that achieves the outcome. Usually connect:
1. A recognizable situation, problem, or mistaken assumption.
2. The user's perspective and why it matters in that situation.
3. A usable mechanism: decision rule, process, diagnostic, demonstration, or explanation.
4. One worked example showing the reasoning, not just categories.
5. A transfer: how the learner applies it to their own case or recognizes the pattern elsewhere.

For procedural advice, specify the trigger, concrete action, and resulting output or check. For decisions, specify criteria and explain why a case belongs in one option. For explanations, connect cause to effect and show what changes under a different condition. Add exceptions only when they materially prevent misuse.

Treat “be strategic,” “add context,” “think critically,” “use AI,” and “choose the right role” as unfinished instructions until the learner knows what to do or look for. Naming a framework does not teach its use.

Use before-and-after demonstrations when they make the lesson easier to see, especially for revisions, prompts, page structure, or work quality. Keep the underlying task and constraints comparable; show the before, the specific intervention, the after, and why the intervention matters. Connect the visible difference to a rule the learner can reuse. Prefer user-provided artifacts; clearly label invented examples as illustrative. Do not present a hypothetical AI answer as an actual model run or a rewrite as proof of ranking, citation, or conversion gains. For judgment-based lessons, a worked decision may be more useful than a before-and-after.

Give a next step appropriate to the lesson: a bounded action or decision, the artifact it produces, and a simple check. Explain how to choose where to start when several actions compete. Keep a quality check distinct from an outcome metric; a clear answer is immediately inspectable, while increased discovery requires separate measurement. Avoid inventing arbitrary numerical targets or an unnecessary checklist.

Preserve distinctive wording, natural spoken rhythm, and first-person perspective where appropriate. Use confident, approachable language. Avoid fake authority, inflated promises, gratuitous acronyms, generic tips, and a forced three-step formula. Keep substance proportional to format; do not make a short video carry a course.

Make the hook promise the actual lesson. When review-hooks is available, apply it after establishing the teaching payoff. Let repurpose-articles govern source fidelity when repurposing an article. Do not replace teaching development with hook optimization.

## 5. Check and deliver

Check internally:
- Can a learner use this on a new case rather than merely repeat labels?
- Does the example demonstrate the rule and explain the choice?
- Does each instruction specify something observable?
- Is the original perspective recognizable?
- Are proposed additions and hypothetical examples distinguishable from user-supplied substance?
- Does the opening earn its promised payoff?
- Is the takeaway clear before the framework is explained?
- Does the next step follow from the lesson, with a way to check the work?
- For existing content, have useful teaching and the original scope been preserved?

Revise failed checks. Avoid a scorecard unless requested.

During discovery, return the proposed lesson, specific gap, and targeted questions. For a review, identify what already teaches well, the highest-impact gap, and a concrete proposed improvement; tie observations to actual passages. Use a small table when it helps compare instruction with missing detail. Avoid an exhaustive scorecard. After sufficient clarification, return a concise learning outcome and complete draft in the requested format. Briefly identify material assumptions or a changed premise. Keep analysis shorter than the content unless asked for coaching.
